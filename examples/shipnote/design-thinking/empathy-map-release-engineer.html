What this suggests they need

Standing in Teo’s shoes: the job is accountability without authority, done late and alone, for readers who only speak up when something breaks. These are team inferences, to carry into Needs Statements.

To get what a PR means from its author while the author still remembers, not on the night before release.

I1 · Team · from T1, T2

For the people who wrote the code to share responsibility for what the notes say, without the release engineer policing colleagues.

I2 · Team · from T2

To know the notes are complete before worrying whether they read well; in regulated teams, provably complete.

I3 · Team · from T4, N25

To know who reads the notes and what each reader needs, including what is safe to leave out.

Interview with SU-3 Teo Abara Simulated
Interviewer

Tell me about the last release you put out. Where did the notes start for you?

SU-3 Teo

Two weeks ago. 4.18, Thursday. Notes start Wednesday around six, after merge freeze. I run the draft, it gives me sixty-three PRs, and thirty-one of them are in Other. So that’s where it starts, really. Other is where PRs go to die.

Interviewer

Walk me through what you did between that first draft and the notes going out.

SU-3 Teo

Go down Other line by line. Open the PR, read the diff if the title’s useless, which is most of them. Fix, feature, internal? Then I move the line in the Markdown. I don’t touch the label on GitHub. It’s faster in the file, and it’s nine at night. Then titles. “fix: null check inv svc” isn’t going to a customer, so I rewrite it. Two hours this time. The draft’s fine. It’s the inputs that are garbage.

Interviewer

Who did you need something from that week? How did you get it?

SU-3 Teo

Authors. I post in #releases: “If your PR is in Other, label it by 3pm Thursday.” Four people did. Out of twenty-something. Hollis, the PM, wants three highlights at the top, so I DM Hollis to find out which three. CS gets a heads-up copy Thursday morning.

Interviewer

What did you have open on your screen while you were working on them?

SU-3 Teo

The Markdown. A tab per PR, so thirty-ish tabs. Slack. And a text file of phrases I paste from. “Fixed an issue where…”, that kind of thing. Jira, if the PR links a ticket. It usually doesn’t.

Interviewer

Tell me about the worst recent moment you’ve had with release notes.

SU-3 Teo

September. PR titled “Remove legacy export.” I left it under Removed because that’s what it said. It was an internal admin export nobody outside ever used. A customer read it, thought their CSV export was going away, called their account manager, who called CS, who called me. Not the author. Me. I’m not the one who knows what that PR does. I’m the one whose name is on the notes. That one still annoys me.

Interviewer

After the notes go out, who reads them, and how do you find out what they thought?

SU-3 Teo

Honestly? No idea until something’s wrong. CS skims the heads-up. Sales reads it when a customer quotes it at them. If nobody complains I assume nobody read it.

Interviewer

Is there anything you do around the notes that nobody asked you to do?

SU-3 Teo

The phrases file. And I cut dependency bumps completely. Nobody asked. Nobody told me not to. Oh, and the one sprint Other came back empty I actually said “yes” out loud. Working from home. To nobody.

Interviewer

What should I have asked that I didn’t?

SU-3 Teo

Why I don’t just make labels required. I’ve asked. Twice. “We don’t want to slow down merges.” So.

Interview with SU-1 Juno Marchetti Simulated
Interviewer

Tell me about the last release you put out. Where did the notes start for you?

SU-1 Juno

Um, last Friday? We do every second Friday. Honestly the notes start on Monday for me, because that’s when I start worrying about them. It’s the one thing I do that customers actually see. But the actual doing is Thursday afternoon. I run the shipnote thing with the version number, like Dana’s runbook says. Dana had the job before me.

Interviewer

Walk me through what you did between that first draft and the notes going out.

SU-1 Juno

I go through every single line. PR open on one side, the Jira ticket on the other, if there is one, and I try to figure out what it means for, like, a person using the app. There were fourteen in Other. I didn’t know what maybe half of them were? Sorry, this is probably really obvious stuff.

Interviewer

Who did you need something from that week? How did you get it?

SU-1 Juno

The engineers who wrote them. I DM them one at a time, because I don’t want to call anyone out in the channel. “Hey, sorry, quick question about your PR, no rush!” And then there is a rush, obviously. Most of them answer eventually. I don’t want to be the label police. They’re way more senior than me.

Interviewer

What did you have open on your screen while you were working on them?

SU-1 Juno

Dana’s runbook, the draft, GitHub, Jira, Slack. And the notes from the release before, so I can copy how they worded things.

Interviewer

Tell me about the worst recent moment you’ve had with release notes.

SU-1 Juno

My very first one. I didn’t know you were supposed to edit the draft. I thought the tool did it. So I published it with Other just… sitting there. Fourteen lines. Our PM messaged me, really nicely, “what is ‘chore: bump deps’?” And it was on the public changelog page. I wanted to disappear. I fixed it in ten minutes, but people had seen it.

Interviewer

After the notes go out, who reads them, and how do you find out what they thought?

SU-1 Juno

The PM. Support, I think? I don’t actually know who else. Nobody really tells me if they’re good. I’d kind of like to know.

Interviewer

Is there anything you do around the notes that nobody asked you to do?

SU-1 Juno

I keep a list of every PR I wasn’t sure about and what I decided, in case someone asks why. Nobody has asked. Mostly I just want to know what’s safe to leave out.

Interviewer

What should I have asked that I didn’t?

SU-1 Juno

Maybe whether there’s a right way to do this? Like, maybe everyone else knows a rule about what goes in, and I just never got told.

Interview with SU-2 Odile Brennan-Kask Simulated
Interviewer

Tell me about the last release you put out. Where did the notes start for you?

SU-2 Odile

Release 7.2, three weeks ago. We release every six weeks. For us the release notes are a controlled document. They begin when the release branch is cut, because from that point I need the complete list of what changed.

Interviewer

Walk me through what you did between that first draft and the notes going out.

SU-2 Odile

I generate the draft to get the list. I don’t let anything write for me. I use it for the list. The PRs go into our release-notes template, a Word document under document control, and beside every line I enter the change request number from our change-control system. Then I check both directions: every PR has a change request, every change request has a PR. That comparison is the part that matters. The wording I write myself, in the approved style.

Interviewer

Who did you need something from that week? How did you get it?

SU-2 Odile

Engineering leads, whenever a PR has no change request. Regulatory affairs reviews and signs, and so does QA. Sign-off happens in our quality system. Not in GitHub, and not in Slack.

Interviewer

What did you have open on your screen while you were working on them?

SU-2 Odile

The template, the change-control system, GitHub, and a spreadsheet I build every release that maps each PR to its change request. Two monitors. It isn’t elegant.

Interviewer

Tell me about the worst recent moment you’ve had with release notes.

SU-2 Odile

Last year’s surveillance audit. The auditor picked one line from release 6.4: “Corrected label text on settings screen.” There was no change request behind it. A developer had merged a one-line typo fix directly. It became a finding, then a CAPA. I spent two weeks on a corrective action because of a one-line typo fix. If it isn’t traceable to a change request, it didn’t happen. That’s how the auditor reads it. I don’t sleep well the week before an audit now.

Interviewer

After the notes go out, who reads them, and how do you find out what they thought?

SU-2 Odile

Regulatory, QA, eventually the auditors, and our customers’ quality teams. Hospitals request them. Customers rarely comment. Auditors always do.

Interviewer

Is there anything you do around the notes that nobody asked you to do?

SU-2 Odile

The spreadsheet. I have one for every release since 5.0. Nobody asked for it. It has saved me twice.

Interviewer

What should I have asked that I didn’t?

SU-2 Odile

You asked about the notes. Ask about the six weeks before them. By the time I see a gap, it’s already merged, and an untraced merge costs far more to fix after the fact than before. That is what wears on me.

Playback

Every other Wednesday evening, Teo inherits about sixty pull requests written by twenty-odd people who have already moved on. Half carry no label, so Teo rebuilds the notes by hand, in the Markdown rather than at the source, because a request to require labels has been turned down twice and it is nine at night. The notes go out under Teo’s name. Nobody mentions them until a line is wrong, and then the call comes to Teo, not to the author. So Teo keeps a file of safe phrases and moves on. Newer release engineers like Juno do the same job while afraid to ask senior colleagues anything. In a regulated shop like Odile’s, a missing line isn’t embarrassing: it’s an audit finding.

Read this map as a hypothesis. 35 of 39 notes are simulated or team assumptions and 2 are open questions. Only 2 come from real users, both support-channel lines quoted in the README. The real-user check that matters most: sit with one real release engineer through their next sprint-end draft and watch what happens to Other.

Key takeaways

  1. The hardest part of the job is the Other cleanup the night before release. It happens in the output because fixing the source hasn’t worked. S2 backs the cause; the rest is simulated.
  2. Release engineers answer for content other people decided, with little authority to ask for better inputs (T2).
  3. Readers stay invisible until something is wrong, so the only feedback is blame (T3).
  4. “Done” splits by context: readable for customers (SU-1, SU-3) versus provably complete for auditors (SU-2, S3).

Validate next

  • Simulated The Other cleanup happens in the Markdown, not on GitHub, and takes hours (N18). Observe one real sprint-end draft.
  • Assumption Release engineers can’t require labels, and pushing harder would cost goodwill (N5, N11). Ask three real release engineers whether they’ve tried and what happened.
  • Simulated Feedback on the notes arrives only when a line is wrong (N16, N29). Ask a real CS or support lead who reads the notes and when they last flagged one.
  • Question Is the regulated release manager (SU-2, S3) the same user, or a second map? Talk to the team behind the S3 sign-off request.
